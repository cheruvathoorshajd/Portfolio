VERITAS lets anyone drop a conversation — spoken, uploaded, or pasted — and watch it get fact-checked in real time. The hard design problem wasn't the checking. It was making a slow, uncertain, six-stage AI process feel legible, honest, and fast enough to trust in the moment.

(01)

The problem

Why it matters

People form beliefs live — during a debate, a podcast, a meeting, a lecture. But verification is always after the fact. By the time a claim is checked, the moment that mattered is gone, and the check lives somewhere the listener will never look.

I looked at how existing fact-checkers actually feel to use, and three friction points kept surfacing.

01

They're opaque

You get a rating, not a reason. There's no visible path from claim to verdict, so there's nothing to trust or disagree with.

02

They're single-format

Text in, rating out. Nobody's meeting people where the claims actually happen — in speech, in documents, in the flow of a real conversation.

03

They lose the speaker

Claims float free of who said them. There's no accountability, and no way to ask "how reliable was this person today?"

The design brief, in one line

Turn a black box into something a person can watch, question, and believe.

Framing · the checking is the AI's job. The trust is the design's job.

The engineering could produce a correct verdict. That alone changes nothing if the person doesn't understand where it came from, can't tell how sure the system is, or has already scrolled past. Every design decision below is aimed at one outcome: calibrated trust — belief that tracks the evidence, not blind faith and not blanket doubt.

(02)

Who it's for

Jobs to be done

Solo project, lightweight research: I ran informal interviews and think-alouds with people who consume claim-heavy media, then wrote two provisional personas to anchor decisions. Both share one job — reduce the effort between hearing a claim and knowing whether to believe it.

Primary · the live listener

Maya — follows debates & podcasts

“When I'm listening to two people argue, I want to know in the moment which one is playing loose with the facts — without pausing to open five tabs.”

  • Claims fly past faster than she can check them
  • Doesn't know which speaker to trust as the conversation goes on
  • Distrusts single ratings with no evidence behind them
Secondary · the analyst

Dev — researcher & note-taker

“I have a 40-minute transcript and a report to write. I need the checkable claims pulled out, sourced, and grouped by who said them — as a starting point, not a black-box answer.”

  • Manual claim extraction from long docs is tedious
  • Needs sources he can click, not a score to take on faith
  • Wants to override the machine when it's uncertain
(03)

Design principles

The north stars

Five principles I set before touching a layout. Every decision downstream had to answer to at least one of them. They doubled as tie-breakers whenever “more information” fought “less friction.”

01

Show the machine thinking

Never a spinner. If the system is working, the person should see it working — stage by stage, claim by claim. Visible process is the foundation of trust.

02

Never overclaim certainty

A confident-looking wrong answer is worse than a hedged one. Confidence is always visible, and genuinely uncertain verdicts get handed to a human rather than faked.

03

Meet people in their format

Claims live in speech, documents, and text. The product accepts all three through one mental model, so nobody has to reshape their input to fit the tool.

04

Attribute everything

Every claim, verdict, and score is bound to a speaker. Accountability isn't a feature bolted on — it's carried through the whole pipeline.

05

Fast to first signal

The first verdict should land as early as possible. Perceived speed comes from streaming results, not from making the whole job finish faster.

Key design decisions

Tension → move → why
D1

Choosing the input model: three concepts, one deck

Tension  ·  Three very different inputs (live audio, files, text) — how do you present them without a cluttered form or a hidden menu?

I designed and prototyped three distinct entry patterns as standalone previews, then pressure-tested each against the principles “meet people in their format” and “fast to first signal.”

Concept ARejected
+

Unified drop zone

One surface for everything — drop a file, click to record, or start typing.

✕Minimal, but ambiguous. Nothing signalled that live audio or paste were even options.
Concept BRejected

Mixer fader

Inputs as channels on an audio mixer — on-brand and playful.

✕Implied you'd blend modes, which you never do. The metaphor fought the mental model.
Concept C◆ Shipped

Trifold deck

Three fanned cards — Mic, Document, Paste. Each an equal choice with its own texture.

◆One tap, zero ambiguity. The only concept that scored on both clarity and delight.

Why the deck won: it makes three unlike inputs feel like three equal, legible doors. Each card carries a texture that previews its mode — a live waveform for the mic, paper grain for documents, a blinking caret for text — so the choice is recognisable before you read a word.

D2

Making an opaque process watchable

Tension  ·  Verification takes real time and many hidden steps. A loading spinner reads as “broken” and kills trust.

Instead of hiding the pipeline behind a wait, I surfaced it. Results stream in as they happen — a transcript line appears, then a claim gets flagged, then a search kicks off, then a verdict lands. The person isn't waiting for an answer; they're watching one get built.

✕ Spinner (rejected)

“Checking…” for 20+ seconds. No signal of progress or life. Users assumed it had frozen and refreshed — losing the run.

◦ Streamed pipeline (shipped)

Six-stage rail + live feed. Every few hundred milliseconds something visibly changes. Perceived wait collapses; the process itself builds credibility.

D3

A verdict system that tells the truth about uncertainty

Tension  ·  Binary true/false is a lie. Real claims are misleading, unverifiable, or genuinely borderline. How do you show that without overwhelming people?

I designed four verdict states, each with a dedicated colour and a plain-language label, plus a visible confidence score and an evidence list. Colour does the fast reading; the score and sources do the trusting.

● Verifiedconfidence 96
Multiple credible sources agree. Green means you can move on.
● Falseconfidence 92
Credible evidence directly contradicts it. Red means stop.
● Misleadingconfidence 71
Technically defensible, materially misleading. Amber means look closer.
● Unverifiedconfidence 14
Not enough evidence exists to rule either way. The system says so plainly instead of guessing.
The most important design decision on this screen

Verdicts that land in the genuinely uncertain 40–70% confidence band aren't presented as answers. They surface an approval control and ask a human to decide — a calibrated human-in-the-loop. The interface's job there is to make the machine's doubt visible and actionable, not to paper over it. Designing the honest, low-confidence state well mattered more than designing the confident one.

D4

Binding every claim to a speaker

Tension  ·  Attribution has to be instantly scannable across a long transcript — and diarization is noisy for monologues.

Each speaker gets a persistent colour, carried from the transcript all the way to a per-speaker accuracy report. You can read a conversation and see, at a glance, whose claims are holding up. Claims are highlighted inline where they occur, so the source sentence and its verdict are never far apart.

Speaker colour is consistent everywhere it appears; claim highlights link a sentence to its verdict without a jump.

The noise problem: diarization splits a single speaker into phantom “speakers” across pauses. For a lecture or podcast that's actively harmful. So I added a Single Speaker / Multi-Speaker toggle — one deliberate choice that trades attribution for cleaner output when there's only one voice. It locks once recording starts, because switching mid-session would corrupt the speaker map.

D5

An editorial-brutalist language — because a truth machine should feel serious

Tension  ·  An AI tool can easily read as a toy or a hype-deck. This one is making claims about what's true. It has to earn sobriety.

I chose a dark, high-contrast, monospace-driven system with hairline rules, numbered section markers, and zero decorative gradients. The register is a scientific instrument, not a consumer app — restraint is the trust signal.

There's a deliberate duality in the type: the machine speaks in monospace (labels, stages, verdicts, metadata) and the human narrative speaks in a serif. It mirrors the product's core idea — an automated pipeline that still defers to human judgment.

A verdict you trust should look like evidence, not marketing.

Every visible element is bespoke — no UI kit in the build. Discipline in spacing and type carries the whole aesthetic, so the one place I spend boldness is the semantic verdict colour.

(07)

What testing changed

Iteration

Informal think-alouds with target users surfaced three trust breaks. Each one changed the design.

✕ Before — bare verdict

Early builds showed a label and a score, nothing else. Users flatly didn't believe it: “Says false — based on what?” The verdict had no standing.

◦ After — evidence attached

Added the source list with stance markers and credibility badges directly on the card. Trust rose immediately; people could check the machine's work.

✕ Before — silent uncertainty

When retrieval came back thin, a weak verdict appeared with no explanation. Users read low confidence as the product being bad, not the evidence being scarce.

◦ After — name the reason

The interface now surfaces a soft warning when a source can't be reached, so a thin verdict reads as “the web was quiet on this,” not “this tool is broken.”

✕ Before — nothing to try

First-time users hit a blank input screen and didn't know what a “good” conversation looked like or what the output would be.

◦ After — one-tap demo

Added a Run Demo path that plays a pre-built conversation through the full pipeline with realistic pacing. People understand the entire product in 20 seconds, no setup.

The design shipped as a working product, not a mockup. On the accuracy side, an evaluation set of hand-labelled claims run through the real pipeline produced a per-label precision/recall/F1 breakdown — the calibration work that lets the confidence design stay honest rather than decorative. On the experience side, the streamed-pipeline and evidence-first patterns consistently moved test users from “I don't trust this” to “show me the sources” — exactly the calibrated-trust posture the project set out to create.

(09)

Reflection & what's next

Looking back

What I'd do differently

Run a formal, moderated usability study earlier — my testing was lightweight, and the trust findings deserved rigour. I'd also commit to a full accessibility pass: the dark, low-chroma palette needs contrast auditing against WCAG, and the colour-coded verdict system needs a non-colour redundancy (shape or label) for colour-blind users. That's the honest gap in a design that leans this hard on colour.

Where it goes next

A fifth Contested verdict state for claims with credible evidence on both sides (the logic exists; the UI is designed but not yet wired). A mobile-first pass — the current experience is desktop-led, but the “live listener” persona is on a phone. And a claim-genealogy view that shows how related claims connect across a long conversation.

(09b)

What this project demonstrates

Designing for the user's moment

Started from the listener's moment, not the model. Three input doors exist because claims live in three formats — the tool bends to the person, not the reverse.

Simplifying complexity

Collapsed an opaque, multi-step AI process into one legible rail and one recognisable verdict card. Complexity absorbed by the system, not the user.

Designing for uncertainty

Designed for calibrated confidence and a human-in-the-loop on uncertain calls — the interface shows doubt instead of faking certainty.

End-to-end ownership

Tested to real trust breaks, iterated the fixes, and shipped a working, evaluated product end-to-end — from research through a bespoke design system in code.
